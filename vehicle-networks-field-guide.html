TL;DR — the car is 5 neighborhoods with very different trust levels

Vehicle zones are neighborhoods, not just parts

A car isn't one giant computer — it's a small city of 50–150 specialized computers (ECUs), grouped into zones by what they do and how much trust they're given. Click a region on the car above; the details appear here.

INFOTAINMENT

The entertainment district

Touchscreen, media, navigation, phone projection (CarPlay/Android Auto), voice assistant. High computing power, frequent software updates, and — critically — the zone most exposed to the outside world through apps, USB, and Bluetooth.

Think of it as: the mall and cinema district of the city — busy, public-facing, constantly changing storefronts, and the place a pickpocket would try to blend in first.
WHY SPLIT INTO ZONES AT ALL?

Two reasons a city planner would recognize:

  • Blast radius. If the infotainment zone gets hacked through a bad app, it should never be able to reach steering or braking.
  • Different needs. ADAS needs microsecond timing; infotainment needs a fast app processor. One zone's hardware would be wrong for the other's job.

A central gateway ECU sits between zones like a city's ring road checkpoints — it decides what traffic is allowed to cross from one district into another.

TL;DR — CAN = priority wins, FlexRay = everyone gets a fixed turn

CAN and FlexRay: two kinds of roads

Zones need roads connecting the ECUs inside them. The two classic ones are CAN and FlexRay — same job (carry short messages between ECUs), very different traffic rules.

CAN — CONTROLLER AREA NETWORK

A single shared road, priority wins

Every ECU on a CAN bus shares one wire pair and can speak whenever it's free. If two speak at once, the message with the more "urgent" ID (lower number) just keeps talking — the other backs off automatically, with zero data lost. No traffic cop needed.

Think of it as: an all-way stop sign where an ambulance always has right-of-way — everyone yields the instant a higher-priority message shows up, then the queue resumes.
lowest ID wins arbitration →
FLEXRAY — TIME-TRIGGERED BUS

A train timetable, not a free-for-all

Every ECU gets a pre-assigned time slot in a repeating cycle. It can only speak in its slot — never early, never late. Nothing is left to chance, so timing is guaranteed, which matters when milliseconds decide whether a steering correction arrives in time.

Think of it as: a train station board — platform 1 departs at :00, platform 2 at :05, always, whether or not anyone is watching. Used where CAN's "whoever's more urgent" model isn't predictable enough (e.g. steer-by-wire, some drivetrain control).
each ECU only talks in its own slot
When to use which: CAN is cheaper and flexible — great for body control, HVAC, seats, most diagnostics. FlexRay costs more but guarantees timing — reserved for a shrinking set of hard-real-time jobs, and in many new platforms it's being replaced by switched Automotive Ethernet running time-sensitive networking instead.
🏎️
Fun fact: CAN's "lowest ID wins" trick is called bitwise arbitration — it works because a dominant bit (0) always beats a recessive bit (1) electrically, so the bus itself resolves the tie in nanoseconds, without a single retry.

TL;DR — services announce themselves, clients subscribe, updates flow only to subscribers

SOME/IP: a food-truck rally, not a fixed timetable

As cars added Ethernet for high-bandwidth zones (cameras, infotainment), CAN-style "everyone hears everything" stopped scaling. SOME/IP (Scalable service-Oriented MiddlewarE over IP) lets ECUs offer and consume named services over Ethernet — call a function remotely, or subscribe to get notified only when something changes.

Think of it as: a food-truck rally. Trucks (servers) roll in and shout their menu ("Rear-Camera-Feed, v2, now serving!"). Hungry apps (clients) don't need to know in advance who's coming — they just listen for the announcement, or ask "anyone serving camera feed?", then walk up and order. If you want fresh updates without asking every minute, you leave your name on the truck's notify list — that's a subscribe/notify, the SOME/IP equivalent of "text me when it's ready."
step 0 of 4
SERVICE DISCOVERY (SD), STEP BY STEP
  1. Offer — server periodically shouts "I serve RearCamera."
  2. Find — a client that just woke up can also ask "who serves RearCamera?" instead of waiting.
  3. Subscribe — client asks to be notified on updates (an "eventgroup").
  4. Notify — server pushes new frames/events only to subscribers, not to the whole network.
WHY NOT JUST BROADCAST LIKE CAN?

A camera stream is far too big to blast to every ECU the way a CAN signal is. SOME/IP's request/response and pub/sub model means bandwidth is spent only where there's an actual subscriber — and services can come and go (a plug-in trailer module, a software update) without reprogramming everyone else's wiring.

🚚
Fun fact: because services are found by name at runtime, a trailer with brake lights and cameras can plug in, announce "I offer TrailerCamera," and the car's software uses it immediately — no firmware update required.

TL;DR — a crypto seal proves who sent it, a rising counter proves it's not a rerun

SecOC: a wax seal and a numbered ticket

CAN and SOME/IP messages, by themselves, don't prove who sent them or stop someone from recording and replaying an old one (imagine replaying an old "unlock door" message). AUTOSAR SecOC (Secure Onboard Communication) bolts two things onto a message: proof of sender, and proof of freshness.

The wax seal — message authentication: Sender and receiver share a secret key, like a signet ring only they own. The sender stamps a MAC (Message Authentication Code) onto the message — a short cryptographic fingerprint over the data + the secret key. Anyone can read the message, but only someone with the matching ring could have made that exact seal. If a single bit changes, the seal breaks.
The numbered ticket — replay protection: Every message also carries a freshness counter that only ever goes up, like a deli counter's ticket numbers. The receiver remembers the last number it accepted and rejects anything at or below that — so a recording of an old, perfectly-sealed message ("ticket #482, unlock door") gets refused because #482 was already served.
ready
Why not encrypt everything instead? SecOC is about authenticity and freshness, not secrecy — most vehicle control messages ("brake pressure = 40%") aren't sensitive to read, but it's critical no one can forge or replay them. That's cheaper than full encryption on tiny, latency-sensitive ECUs, which is why SecOC layers a MAC + freshness counter on top of the existing CAN/SOME-IP payload rather than hiding it.
🎟️
Fun fact: real SecOC deployments don't send the full counter with every message — that would waste bandwidth. They send a few low bits and periodically resync the full value, trusting both sides to track the count in between.
Quick check: an attacker perfectly records a valid "unlock door" message and replays it later. Does SecOC stop it?

Putting it all together: one message's trip

Say a corner radar (ADAS zone) detects a pedestrian and needs the infotainment cluster to flash a warning. Here's every concept above, in one path.

  1. Zone: the radar lives in the ADAS zone, kept isolated from infotainment for safety.
  2. Bus: inside the ADAS zone, the alert goes out as a high-priority CAN message — its low ID wins arbitration instantly over routine traffic.
  3. SecOC: before it leaves the zone, the message is stamped with a MAC and a freshness counter, so the gateway can trust it's genuine and current.
  4. Gateway: the central gateway checks the seal, checks the zone-crossing rules, and forwards it toward the infotainment zone.
  5. SOME/IP: on the Ethernet backbone, this becomes an event notify to every ECU that previously subscribed to "pedestrian-alerts" — likely the infotainment cluster and any recording/telematics service.
  6. Result: the dash flashes a warning within milliseconds, and nothing on the infotainment side could have reached back into ADAS to cause this — the trust only flows one way.
